# Urban warfare: what US doctrine says (2026-10-15)

The evidence behind [urban-design.md](urban-design.md). Every quote was found by text search in the corpus (`tools/doctrine.py`, release 20261009-211022 as re-published 2026-10-14). The 90%-within-50 m, the depth-against-sector trade (para 4-53) and the clearing times (para 3-8) were re-checked on the text. Cells marked ⚠ are extraction doubts: check them against the PDF before a rule rests on them.

Manuals used: ATP 3-06 (2022) Urban Operations; ATP 3-06.11 (2024) BCT Urban Operations; ATTP 3-06.11 (2011)
Combined Arms Operations in Urban Terrain; TC 3-21.76 (2025) Ranger Handbook; TC 3-21.75 (2013) Warrior Ethos and
Soldier Combat Skills (the urban individual-movement chapter that ATP 3-06.11 points to); ATP 3-21.90 (2019) mortars;
FM 3-90 (2023) for task definitions.

Coverage notes (read first):
- **ATP 3-21.8 (2024) platoon/squad and ATP 3-21.10 (2026) company carry almost no urban material.** ATP 3-21.10 sends the
  reader away: "Urban terrain (see ATP 3-06.11 and ATP 3-06 for more information)" — ATP 3-21.10 (2026), para 1-6.
  ATP 3-21.8 holds only battle drill 6 (Enter and Clear a Room) by reference and a few weapon notes. The usable urban
  doctrine is ATP 3-06.11 (2024), backed by ATTP 3-06.11 (2011) for the older techniques.
- Paragraph numbers are as the corpus chunks carry them. Where a figure sits in a table or bulleted list with no paragraph
  number, the citation gives the table, or the heading and page.
- Corpus extraction defects are flagged inline (⚠). Check those against the PDF before you build a rule on them.
- ATP 3-09.32 (risk-estimate distances) is **not in the corpus**. Urban doctrine refers to it for danger-close
  figures and gives none of its own.

## 1. Positions inside buildings

**Back from the window, in the room's shadow.**
- "locates them back from the windows in the shadows of the room" — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 6-50 (hasty defense).
- "Soldiers should position themselves deeper into the building so that weapon barrels and muzzle blasts do not project
  close to or beyond the edge of the window to reduce chances of detection." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-51.
- "Soldiers are better concealed if they fire from the shadowed interior of a room." — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), camouflage
  list, pdf p. 197.
- Machine guns "are best employed inside a building, taking full advantage of interior shadows" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024),
  para 4-62.

**The trade-off between depth and sector, stated by doctrine:**
- "The farther back into a structure a Soldier is, the better the protection and the smaller the weapon signature.
  However, the sector of fire is more limited." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-53.
- "Most building apertures have a very narrow field of fire laterally and vertically, but units can add more positions to
  ensure interlocked fires" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-51.

**Firing ports and loopholes:**
- Barricade every opening and keep two or three ports in each: "It is better to leave two or three firing ports in each
  door and window." Firing from the bottom of the window "gives the Soldier the advantage of the adjoining walls" —
  ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-55.
- "Barricade all ground-floor doors and windows unless they are critical for friendly movement." Barricade enough
  upper-floor windows "to create vertical depth" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-54.
- Fortified loophole: on the second floor or higher, "place two layers of sandbags on the floor under the Soldier", with
  a rear wall and overhead cover — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-56. Dummy loopholes and varied heights aid concealment —
  para 4-57.
- The size of a loophole is defined: "Loopholes are firing apertures (a minimum of 8 inches in diameter)" — ATP 3-06.11
  (2024), para B-3.

**Which floor:**
- Upper floors are for vertical depth and anti-armour. They "enable forces to place dismounted weapon systems vertically
  ... using upper floor windows, balconies, and roofs", and anti-armour teams on higher floors gain effectiveness "while
  reducing enemy ability to return fire" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-50. The same paragraph warns against "massing
  forces in the same structure as its destruction puts them all at risk".
- Machine guns: "Often, the second floor is the best compromise since it is above common debris, allows for relatively
  low trajectory fire, and the floors above provide indirect fire protection." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-29.
- Firing from upper stories "protects the shooter from tank main gun and coaxial machine gunfire" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024),
  para B-49. Tank main-gun limits are −10° to +20° — para B-96.
- Rooftops are usually for snipers only: "A rooftop position is usually reserved for snipers" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024),
  para 4-59. A rooftop position "is fully exposed to both indirect fires and aerial fires" — para 4-61. In a strongpoint,
  avoid "defending from roof-top positions due to the threat of air-burst munitions or attack by loitering munitions" —
  ATP 3-06.11 (2024), Defend a Strong Point list, pdf p. 122.
- Basements and masonry: "Mason and stone buildings with basements and cellars protect Soldiers/Marines from most fires
  except the largest caliber or tonnage munitions." — ATP 3-06 (2022), para 5-7.
- Anti-armour weapons need backblast vent area: "at least 20 square feet of open area, such as a standard 3-foot by
  7-foot doorway" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), backblast considerations, pdf p. 198. Some need "an ample opening of at least 10
  feet by 15 feet" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-63.

**What a firer can see, and be seen from:**
- Street width governs whether you can see into windows across the street: "When a street is narrow, observing or firing
  into windows of a building across the street can be difficult because an observer is forced to look along the
  building rather than into windows." — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para A-60.
- Interior visibility is poor: "Smoke, dust, shadows, and the lack of light penetrating inner rooms all combine to reduce
  visibility" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-21.

**Numbers: none for distance from the window.** Doctrine says "back", "deeper" and "in the shadows" but gives no metres
or sector width. The only interior distances it gives are 12–18 in from walls while moving (see Q3) and the backblast
figures above.

## 2. Windows, loopholes, walls: breaching and protection

**Hole sizes are defined.** Loophole: min 8 in diameter — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-3. "A mousehole is usually a
minimum of 24 by 30 inches in size." — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para B-4. "A breach hole is a minimum of 50 inches by 30
inches in size." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-4.

**What stops 5.56 mm at under 50 m** (the classic list): "Single row of well-packed sandbags. Layer of tightly packed
books 18 to 24 inches thick. Two-inch concrete wall (nonreinforced). Cinder block filled with sand. Single layer of
brick." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-27. The same paragraph: "an interior wall made of thin wood paneling, sheetrock, or
plaster is no protection against 5.56-mm rounds".
- 5.56 mm range effect: "Maximum penetration occurs at 200 meters. At ranges less than 25 meters, penetration is greatly
  reduced." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-26.
- 7.62 mm: "At 50 meters, the 7.62-mm ball round cannot reliably penetrate a single layer of well-packed sandbags. At 200
  meters, it can penetrate a single sandbag layer, but not a double layer." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-31.
- .50 cal: "penetrates 14 inches of sand or 28 inches of packed earth at 200 meters"; single shots are best for a
  loophole, 3–5-round U-pattern bursts for a breach — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-33. It defeats common barriers except
  "a 55-gallon drum filled with sand, a car engine block, and sewer covers" — para B-34.
- Machine guns in general: "Continued and concentrated machine gunfire can breach most urban walls, but typically cannot
  breach thick reinforced concrete walls or dense natural stone block walls." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-30.
- Hand-grenade fragments "cannot penetrate a single layer of sandbags, a cinder block, or a brick building, but they can
  penetrate wood frame and tin buildings" — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), Table B-1.

**Rounds to penetrate, by weapon and material: Table B-2** ("U.S. and enemy small-arms weapons penetration against
common urban materials"). ATP 3-06.11 (2024), Table B-2, p. 190 (identical in ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), Table B-2):

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| M16/M4 | 35 | 70 | 60 | 35 | 90 | 1-3 | 220 | 1 |
| M249 | 35 | 70 | 60 | 35 | 90 | 1-3 | 220 | 1 |

reinforced concrete; M249 is identical to M16). Check against the PDF before you rely on single cells.

**25 mm cannon (Bradley), rounds to make a loophole / breach hole:**
- APDS-T — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), Table B-8: "3-inch brick wall" 22/75 rounds at 0° and 22/35 at 45°; 8-inch reinforced
  concrete 22/75 at 0° and 22/40 at 45° ("Reinforcing rods still in place"). APDS-T "can penetrate over 16 inches of
  reinforced concrete with enough energy left to cause enemy casualties" — para B-119.
- HEI-T — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), Table B-10 (ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), Table B-11): 3-inch brick 10/20 at 0° and 20/25 at 45°;
  8-inch reinforced concrete 15/25 at 0° and 15/30 at 45°.
- ⚠ "3-inch brick" is the corpus text. It may be an extraction error for a thicker (e.g. triple) brick wall; check the
  PDF.

**Shoulder-launched munitions against walls:**
- They make small holes: "Typically, a small hole is made in the structure, and only those enemy personnel directly in
  the path of the spall fragmentation from the HE round become casualties." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-54.
- By material — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-57 list. Stone: "The AT4 usually does not penetrate a heavy European-style
  stone wall." Brick: "Weapons such as the AT4 may require three to five rounds to penetrate brick walls." Wood: "A
  single round produces a breach hole and significant spall."
- Aim point: "fire at a point 6 to 12 inches from the edge of the top or side edge of the berm or wall" — ATP 3-06.11
  (2024), para B-57.
- M141 BDM: "breach up to 8 inches of reinforced concrete, breach up to 12-inch triple brick walls" — ATP 3-06.11
  (2024), para B-73. "It takes more than one round to create a man-size hole. Use pair or volley fire, placing the rounds
  about 12- to 18- inches apart." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), Table B-7.

**Tank main gun against buildings:**
- MPAT-T: "Impact against concrete walls yield holes of about 24 inches in diameter, but reinforcing bars ... are not
  likely to be cleared" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-103. It arms "approximately 30 meters from the muzzle" — para
  B-102.
- HE-OR-T "has a rubbleing capability" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-106.
- Older guidance: "HEAT ammunition will open a larger hole in reinforced concrete or masonry structures than ... MPAT";
  HEAT "loses most of its effectiveness against urban targets at ranges of less than 60 feet" — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011),
  para 8-18.
- A tank can breach by ramming — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-109.

**Mouse-holing and wall breach as technique:**
- In the defence: "Open passageways in walls as required to facilitate rapid movement to alternate positions within a
  structure or to escape to a different one" (avoid load-bearing walls) — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-52.
- Engineers "can use C4 and other explosives to make firing ports, mouseholes, and demolition obstacles" — ATTP 3-06.11
  (2011), para 6-63.
- In the attack, wall breaches "generally requir[e] the use of main gun rounds, missiles, or large quantities of
  explosives" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-53.
- **Numbers: none for demolition charge size.** Doctrine defers to TM 3-34.82 / TC 3-34.85: a breaching charge is
  "specific to the situation" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 6-99.

## 3. Movement in urban terrain

**Streets and open areas:**
- "Open areas, such as streets, alleys, and parks, should be avoided. They are natural kill zones for enemy,
  crew-served weapons, or snipers." Cross them under smoke — TC 3-21.75 (2013), para 8-1.
- Moving along a building: "maintaining at least 12 inches of separation between yourself and the wall to avoid rabbit
  rounds" — TC 3-21.75 (2013), para 8-2. Elsewhere "12 to 18 inches away from walls" — TC 3-21.75 (2013), para 8-10;
  ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-79.
- Windows: stay below first-floor windows; "step or jump pass the window" for basement windows — TC 3-21.75 (2013),
  paras 8-3 and 8-4.
- General rush across open ground (not urban-specific): "use the 3 to 5 second rush" — TC 3-21.75 (2013), paras 7-4 to
  7-6.
- Fire teams use a diamond "with the rear Soldier focused on threats above" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 3-16.
- Prefer moving through buildings: "Forces should maximize movement within buildings, though this may require breaching
  at a greater cost in supplies, damage to structures, and loss of tempo." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 3-17.
- Night gives less advantage than usual: ambient light "can be great enough to negate the advantage of night vision
  devices" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 3-17.

**Inside buildings:**
- "the base element for moving inside structures remains the four-Soldier fire team. Fire teams ... can only secure
  where they are, not where they have been." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-79.
- Avoid rooms next to exterior walls with windows — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-79.
- Hallway formations: serpentine and rolling-T — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), paras C-80 to C-82.

**Top-down versus ground-floor entry:**
- "clearing a building from the top down is best, because assaulting and defending are easier from upper floors" —
  TC 3-21.75 (2013), para 8-12.
- Why: "a unit's momentum is greater clearing down than up; grenades are easier to throw down a stairwell than up" —
  ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 7-148.
- But in practice "the most common method of gaining entry to a building remains by way of the ground floor" — ATTP
  3-06.11 (2011), para 7-149.
- Roof entry "is less physically demanding and can allow elements to approach defensive positions from the rear", but
  "force size is more limited" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-30.
- A stairwell seized to the top floor gives the same top-down benefit "with the option to keep an open lane to resupply
  and reinforce" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-110.
- Entry "should be made at the highest level possible" — TC 3-21.75 (2013), para 8-11. In high-threat situations, avoid
  ground-floor windows and doors "except as a last resort" — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 7-16.

**Time and manpower to clear: what doctrine gives.** The clearing times
below are one illustrative sentence ("For example, it may take..."), with
no building size, enemy or source, and in no other manual: not a planning
figure (corrected 2026-10-16).
- "it may take a supported platoon 40-60-minutes to clear a one-floor building with a basement. It may take 90-minutes
  to clear a one-floor building with no support and days to clear a multi-story building." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024),
  para 3-8.
- "UO building clearing is not fast. Building clearing is a slow methodical process." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 3-8.
- "In general, the assault force should consist of at least one Infantry squad per floor to maintain momentum during the
  clearance." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-3. "the clearance is very Soldier-intensive" — same paragraph.
- Defensible space multiplies: "Including the rooftop, a four-story building has about five times the defensible area its
  footprint would suggest" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 1-34.
- City scale: "As a planning factor, a city of 50,000 people will require a BCT to clear, whereas a city of 100,000 will
  require a division." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), Table A-1 (settlement classifications). Settlement sizes: a homestead is
  "the largest settlement that a platoon can manage unaided", a hamlet the largest for a company, a village the largest
  for a battalion — same table.
- Advance rate: higher headquarters "may measure unit progress as the advance of a combined arms battalion in mere tens to
  hundreds of meters per day, rather than ... kilometers" — ATP 3-06 (2022), para 3-91.
- Casualty cost of clearing: "Casualties are normally higher for building clearances than other types of operations." —
  ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-8. **No number is given.**
- Fatigue and rotation: leaders "should have a plan to rotate fresh units into an extended fight" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024),
  para 3-18.
- **Numbers: none for rooms per hour or rooms per squad.** The 40–60 / 90 min figures and "one squad per floor" are the
  only clearing-rate figures found.

## 4. Battle drill: Enter and Clear a Room (07-SQD-D9509)

Sources: TC 3-21.76 (2025), ch. 8, battle drill 07-SQD-D9509 (pdf pp. 197–200), and app. A para A-4; ATP 3-06.11 (2024),
app. C; ATP 3-21.8 (2024) lists it as Battle Drill 6.

- **Conditions:** the squad "has support and security elements positioned at the initial foothold and outside the
  building" — TC 3-21.76 (2025), drill 07-SQD-D9509, Conditions. **Standard:** clear and secure the room "while
  minimizing friendly casualties, noncombatant casualties, and collateral damage", and "maintains a sufficient fighting
  force to repel an enemy counterattack" — same drill, Standards.
- **Team size:** "The base element for clearing rooms is the four-Soldier fire team. ... a Soldier should never attempt
  to clear a room alone if avoidable." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-32.
- **Steps (summary):**
  1. The squad leader positions to control, posts a **security team on corridors and hallways**, picks the entry point
     and orders the clear.
  2. The team leader (normally #2) picks the entry method and signals. In high intensity a grenade goes first ("FRAG
     OUT / STUN OUT", per ROE and construction).
  3. #1 enters and goes left or right "along the path of least resistance to one of two corners". #2 follows at once and
     goes the opposite way.
  4. #3 goes opposite #2.
  5. #4 goes opposite #3.
  6. All engage with precision fire and identify noncombatants.
  7. The team leader announces "CLEAR" and establishes security.
  8. The squad leader keeps the hallway secured.
  9. The squad leader enters, assesses, reports, requests sustainment, marks the entry point.
  10. The squad consolidates and reorganizes.

  — TC 3-21.76 (2025), drill 07-SQD-D9509, task steps 1–10. "SHORT ROOM" stops #3 and #4 from entering — same drill.
- **Points of domination:** "The corners of a room are the preferred points of domination." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para
  C-66. "Most rooms have four points of domination based on the point of entry—the two corners on either side of the
  door and at least one meter to the left and to the right of the entry point." — para C-67. Ranger #3 moves "at least 1
  meter (3 feet) from the entry point" — TC 3-21.76 (2025), para A-4.
- **Pace:** movement is "at a brisk walk"; members "stop moving only after they clear the door and reach their designated
  point of domination" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-71. Stay "within one meter of the wall" — para C-72.
- **Fatal funnel:** the entryway "is the most dangerous location"; "a single explosive round or burst from a machine gun
  can kill an entire team in a stack" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), paras C-24 and C-39.
- **Stand-off before entry:** "The clearing team members stand 1 to 2 feet (about 0.5 meters) from the entry point" — TC
  3-21.76 (2025), para 11-27.
- **Marking:** green chemlight means "room clear" — TC 3-21.76 (2025), para 11-31.

**Platoon/company organization for a building:**
- "A force planning to clear a building will organize like a standard attack with designated forces for security, main
  body (including assault, support, and breach elements), reserve, and sustainment." The assault force is larger and
  the support force smaller than usual — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-3.
- "Typically, a squad or platoon conducts the actual assault of an individual building." — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para
  5-88. When a company clears systematically, "one or two platoons may attack on a narrow front ... The other platoon
  supports the clearing units and is prepared to assume their mission." — para 5-94.
- Foothold first: "Upon deciding to clear a building, the immediate goal is to secure a foothold. This generally requires
  an initial entry into the building and clearance of the first room." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-22.

**The sequence.** ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 2-70 lists the deliberate urban attack phases: "Phase 1. Reconnoiter the
objective. Phase 2. Move to the objective. Phase 3. Isolate the objective. Phase 4. Secure a foothold. Phase 5. Clear the
objective. Phase 6. Consolidate and reorganize. Phase 7. Prepare for future missions."
- Isolate: "seizing terrain that dominates the objective so that the enemy cannot resupply, reinforce, or withdraw its
  defenders" — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 5-84.
- Foothold: "A large building is used as a foothold into an urban area, while a specific room is assigned as a foothold
  into a large building." — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 5-87.
- Support element: it suppresses the objective and adjacent buildings, and "destroys or captures any enemy trying to exit
  the building" — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 5-90. Suppressive fire should shift "at the last possible moment" — para
  5-88.
- Clear: "units proceed to clear all floors, to include the basement" — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 5-97.
- Consolidate: the assault force must "repel enemy counterattacks and ... prevent the enemy from infiltrating back into
  the cleared building" — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 5-98.
- ATP 3-06.11 (2024), paras 3-65 to 3-93 restates this at BCT scale (reconnoitre, approach, isolate or fix, finish), with
  "The primary form of maneuver for an urban assault is the penetration." — para 3-93.

## 5. Offence in a town (company/platoon): isolation, foothold, support by fire, force ratios, tempo

**Force ratios. The key numbers:**
- "units can expect to need three to five times the normal (historical ratio) number of assigned forces before
  accounting for additional elements needed to manage the civilian population." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 3-22.
- "Offensive UOs typically require a minimum of three to five times the force ratios needed for rural combat." — ATP 3-06
  (2022), para 2-5. Also "at least three to five times the number of forces, echelons, and equipment" — ATP 3-06 (2022),
  para 2-21.
- ATP 3-06 (2022), Table 5-1, "Typical and urban operations planning force ratios" (adapted from ATP 2-01.3, Table B-16),
  friendly:enemy:

| Mission | Open/rural | Urban (restrictive) |
|---|---|---|
| Delay | 1:7 | 3:7 to 5:7 |
| Defend (prepared) | 1:3 | 1:1 to 5:3 |
| Defend (hasty) | 1:2.5 | 3:2 to 2:1 |
| Attack (hasty positions) | 2.5:1 | 8:1 to 13:5 ⚠ |
| Attack (prepared position) | 3:1 | 9:1 to 15:1 |
| Counterattack (flank) | 1:1 | 3:1 to 5:1 |

  ⚠ "13:5" (=2.6:1) cannot be the upper bound of a 3–5× multiple of 2.5:1. It is probably an extraction error for
  ~12.5:1 or 13:1; check the PDF. The other rows are the open ratio ×3 to ×5.
- Point of penetration (general, not urban-specific): "Combat power is allocated to the assault force to achieve a
  minimum 3:1 ratio on the point of penetration." — ATP 3-21.8 (2024), para 6-19.
- Strongpoints need "overwhelming force at the point of penetration" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 3-23.
- Fires: urban operations "benefit from additional allocation of three to five times the fire support assets, precision
  systems, and munitions" — ATP 3-06 (2022), para 3-54.
- Example: a city of about 50,000 (5 × 7 km, "roughly 20 x 80 blocks") "would require a minimum of two fully equipped
  combat brigades" to attack and seize — ATP 3-06 (2022), para 5-59.

**Isolation and support by fire:**
- "Units will select the best terrain and urban structures that provide the ability to place suppressive fire on the
  objective." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 3-79. "Armored forces are optimal for executing isolation operations" — same
  paragraph.
- Ideally isolate the building, the block, or the whole city: "Ideally, the individual objective building as well as the
  city block or even the complete city is isolated." — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 5-85.
- Support-by-fire definition: "a tactical mission task in which a unit engages the enemy by direct fire in support of
  another maneuvering force" — FM 3-90 (2023), para B-58.
- Bypass and selective clearing: with large open areas between buildings "the commander clears only those buildings along
  the approach to the objective or only those necessary for security" — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 5-94. But "bypassing
  buildings increases the risk of attack from the rear or flank" — para 5-93.

**Tempo:** "The density of urban terrain ... lend[s] to a slower, more deliberate pace" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 3-8,
with the clearing times quoted in Q3. Urban operations "have a slower pace and tempo" — TC 3-21.76 (2025), para 11-17.
- ATP 3-06 (2022), Tables 5-3 and 5-4 (opposed rates of advance, km/h, from Dupuy/ATP 5-0.2-1) exist. ⚠ **Their data rows
  did not extract**; only the headers and notes are in the corpus. The notes say: "Sustained rates of advance are not
  possible without a ratio of 3:1, except in very rare instances usually involving complete surprise." The surprise
  factors are ×5 complete, ×3 substantial, ×1.3 minor. Pull the cells from the PDF if needed.

## 6. Defence in a town

**Frontages and depth. Numbers:**
- "an infantry company, which might occupy 1,500–2,000 meters in an open terrain defense, usually occupies a frontage of
  300–800 meters in urban areas." — ATP 3-06 (2022), para 5-59.
- ATP 3-06 (2022), Table 5-2, "Approximate defensive frontages and depths" (in blocks; "Average block is 175 meters (191
  yards)"): battalion 4–8 frontage × 3–6 depth; company 3–4 × 2–3; platoon 1–2 × 1.

**Strongpoints and mutually supporting buildings:**
- Platoons "receive a small group of buildings in which to prepare their defense, permitting the platoon leader to
  establish mutually supporting, squad-size positions" — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 6-55.
- In a strongpoint: "Ensuring that mutually supporting strong points are echeloned in depth"; divide it "into several
  independent but mutually supporting positions"; "Placing crew served and anti-tank weapons on higher floors" — ATP
  3-06.11 (2024), para 4-122 list.
- Cost: "A minimally effective strong point typically requires one day of effort from an engineer unit the same size as
  the unit defending the strong point." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-119.
- Small elements can hold for long periods: "historical examples of small elements in strongpoints denying attackers for
  weeks or months" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 6-78.
- Settlements as strongpoints: hamlets and villages "can make an effective strongpoint defense or blocking position" —
  ATP 3-06.11 (2024), Table A-1.

**Edge of the village or in depth:**
- "Normally, defending on the leading edge ... is more effective against an armor-heavy force. Defending in depth within
  the village to deny the enemy a foothold is more effective against a force that is primarily infantry." — ATTP
  3-06.11 (2011), para 6-53.
- Lay belts of obstacles: "Barriers and obstacles are emplaced in multiple belts." Use cratering charges at key street
  locations — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 6-62. In a hasty defence, "two belts of barriers and obstacles" — para 6-51.
- Close-in protective obstacles: "concrete barriers and tactical wire"; "fortify all structures involved in the defense,
  denying access to doors, windows, stairwells" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-46.
- Engagement areas are small: "urban engagement areas tend to be small with limited line of sight, defending forces risk
  having the enemy bypass or envelop their fighting positions" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-52.
- Rubbling: "rubbing [rubbling] the buildings too soon or rubbing too many may disclose his exact locations and destroy
  cover" — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 6-60.
- The defender's advantage: urban terrain enables "small units to hold against forces many times their size" — ATP
  3-06.11 (2024), ch. 4 introduction.

**Which buildings: no scoring rule.** Doctrine gives considerations: mutually supporting groups, masonry/basement
protection (ATP 3-06 (2022), para 5-7), structural soundness for vehicles (ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 6-59), fire hazard
(ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-122). The check is to review appendices A and B "to determine if a wall provides suitable
protection" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 4-27.

## 7. Indirect fire and direct HE in towns

**Mortars over artillery:**
- "Mortars are the most used indirect fire weapon in urban combat for their ability to engage targets with a high rate of
  fire, steep angle of fall, and short minimum range." — ATP 3-21.90 (2019), para 4-162. The same point is made in ATP
  3-06.11 (2024), para B-166.
- Final protective fires inside a town: "mortars are more effective than artillery. Mortars have a higher angle of fall,
  which gives them a greater chance of impacting on the street." — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para 6-61.
- **Dead space behind buildings. Numbers:** "For low-angle fire, dead space that cannot be struck is about five times the
  height of the building behind which the target sits. For high-angle fire, dead space is only about one-half the height
  of the building" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-165; also ATP 3-06 (2022), para 3-65. "Intervening buildings that stand
  three or more stories tall hinder close indirect fire support." — ATP 3-06 (2022), para 3-65.
- A mortar firing above 885 mils to clear a frontal mask reduces the counter-battery threat — ATP 3-21.90 (2019), para
  4-164.

**Effect on roofs and floors. Numbers:**
- 60 mm: "cannot penetrate most rooftops even with a delay setting"; penetrates "nearly 4 inches of reinforced concrete"
  — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-169.
- 81 mm: "can penetrate the roofs of light buildings"; "up to 6 inches of reinforced concrete" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024),
  para B-170.
- 120 mm: "up to 12 inches of reinforced concrete"; a direct hit is "equivalent to almost 10 pounds of explosive
  material" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-171.
- Concrete roofs: "Mortar rounds with delay fuses can penetrate the top floor. However, additional penetration is rare
  since each additional floor is also concrete." Interior damage "is significant in the room of impact but limited
  outside that room" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-168.
- Proximity fuze against building fronts: "Shell fragments normally will not penetrate walls. Most casualties are caused
  by the secondary hazard of flying glass." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-164.
- Fires against buildings generally: they "are far less capable of affecting targets within buildings or underground";
  destroying the enemy brings "a greatly increased ammunition consumption rate" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 3-36.
  "Massed artillery ... is generally ineffective against enemy forces within reinforced structures." — para 3-38.

**Artillery, including direct fire. Numbers:**
- "HE 155-mm rounds can penetrate up to 38 inches of brick and nonreinforced concrete and up to 28 inches of reinforced
  concrete". With a concrete-piercing fuze, "One round can penetrate up to 46 inches. Five rounds are needed to create a
  1.5-meter breach in a 1-meter-thick wall. About 10 rounds are needed to create such a breach in a wall 1.5 meters
  thick." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-174.
- "Even with heavy artillery, large expenditures of ammunition are required to knock down buildings of any size." — ATP
  3-06.11 (2024), para B-174.
- 105 mm can "penetrate single layer stone and brick walls or lightweight reinforced concrete" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para
  B-173.
- Choice by structure: HE "give[s] good results against all lightly built structures. For more sturdy structures, the
  105-mm artillery and 120-mm mortar are best, while well-built reinforced concrete structures may require 155-mm" — ATP
  3-06.11 (2024), para B-155.
- Units "may require field artillery for direct fire at targets ranging fewer than a hundred meters" — ATP 3-06 (2022),
  para 3-90.

**Tank:** main gun −10°/+20°. "The lower depression limit creates a 35-foot (10.8-meter) dead space around the tank. On
a 16-meter-wide street, this dead space extends to the buildings on each side" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-96.
Overpressure from the 120 mm "can seriously injure a dismounted Soldier within a 90-degree arc extending from the muzzle
... out to 200 meters" — para B-110. Sabot petals are a hazard "from 200 to 1,000 meters along a tank's line of fire on
a frontage of about 400 meters" — para B-111. Main-gun breaching is covered in Q2.

**Rubble and duds:**
- "An urban area often becomes more of an obstacle to advancing troops and a stronger position for defending troops after
  being reduced to rubble by weapons fire." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-5.
- "Rubble can provide substantial cover for dismounted Soldiers and act as a severe obstacle to vehicular movement." —
  ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-151.
- "When rounds strike a glancing blow on a hard surface, 25 percent of impact-fused explosive rounds may not detonate." —
  ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-19. ATP 3-06 (2022), para 1-8 adds: "up to 25 percent of rounds miss desired points of
  impact due to urban terrain".
- The 120 mm mortar crater "is not deep or steep-sided enough to block vehicular movement" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para
  B-171.

**Danger close / minimum safe distance: no urban-specific number.** Doctrine says close ranges "can put friendly forces
danger close" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 3-33. It also says reduced engagement ranges "can put Soldiers within risk
estimate distances at the start of an engagement" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), fires-considerations list in the chunk carrying para 4-48, pdf p. 109.
The distances themselves are in ATP 3-09.32, which is not in the corpus.

## 8. Line of sight and engagement ranges

- "Close combat is the predominant characteristic of urban engagements with approximately 90 percent of all engagements
  at a range of 50 meters or less." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-15. In the 2011 manual: "About 90 percent of all
  targets are engaged at a range of 50 meters or less." — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para B-16.
- Room fighting: "engagements are very close (within 10 meters) and very fast (with targets exposed for a few seconds
  only)" — TC 3-21.76 (2025), para 11-20.
- Armour: "Standard engagements are typically 200 meters or less—proximity to targets allows for successful coaxial
  machine gun engagements." — ATP 3-06 (2022), para 4-20.
- Indirect-fire targets: "Point targets are less than 200 meters wide. They are the most common type of target due to
  the restricted sight lines ... typical kill zones being no wider than a single street or building." Linear targets are
  200–600 m along streets — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), paras B-148 and B-149.
- Long sight lines exist only along axes: "Short engagement ranges; limited line of sight except down straight roads." —
  ATP 3-06.11 (2024), Table A-4 (street patterns). Long-range fires are possible "down streets or rail lines and across
  parks or plazas" — para B-80.
- Missile minimum ranges matter: Javelin "minimum engagement range is 65 meters in direct attack mode and 150 meters in
  top-attack mode" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-78. TOW minimum arming distance is 65 m — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para
  B-84.
- A tall building gives long LOS: "A tall structure on its own or on the edge of an area can provide line of sight across
  a great distance." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 2-44.
- Targets are fleeting: enemy "present only fleeting targets" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para B-14. "targets (even those at
  close range) tend to be indistinct" — para B-21.

## 9. Ammunition consumption and casualties compared with open terrain

**Doctrine is mostly qualitative here.** The only multiples are force and fire-support multiples (3–5×) and one
sustainment figure.
- Sustainment: "With an expected 20–30 percent expenditure increase in personnel, fuel, ammunition, barrier or obstacle
  material, and end item recovery or reconstitution" — ATP 3-06 (2022), para 3-77. **This is the only numeric
  consumption multiple found.**
- "Urban assaults consume more supplies and produce more casualties than is typical of similar operations in other
  environments." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 3-76.
- "Ammunition expenditure is high when fighting in an urban area." Positions should stockpile ammunition — ATTP 3-06.11
  (2011), para 6-40.
- "Forces conducting UO tend to consume supplies at greater rates. Ammunition is normally the biggest concern" — ATP
  3-06.11 (2024), para 1-63. Vehicle fuel use tends to "stay constant or less overall" — same paragraph.
- "a tank only gets about eight-hours of use on a full tank before needing to refuel, and they can consume ammunition even
  faster" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 3-53.
- Hand grenades "are the most used explosive munitions during intense combat in urban areas" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para
  B-35.
- Fire-support assets: three to five times — ATP 3-06 (2022), para 3-54 (see Q5).
- Casualties: "Unit casualty rates can quickly exceed planner expectations when fighting in urban areas." — ATP 3-06.11
  (2024), para 7-43. "Casualties are normally higher for building clearances than other types of operations." — para C-8.
- Historical anchor, not a planning factor: at Hue in 1968 "many company-sized units suffered more than 60 percent
  casualties in only a few days of offensive operations" — ATP 3-06 (2022), para 2-44.
- Infantry without armour: "Infantry organizations operating without armored vehicle support can lead to heavy
  casualties." — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para 2-106.
- **No casualty-rate multiple (e.g. "N× open terrain") is given anywhere in the corpus.**

## 10. Measures of success: seize, clear, secure, "cleared"

- **Clear**: "Clear is a tactical mission task in which a unit eliminates all enemy forces within an assigned area." This
  means destroying, capturing or forcing the withdrawal of enemy forces "so they cannot execute organized resistance" —
  FM 3-90 (2023), para B-16. The commander may limit it to enemy "larger than a stated size" — para B-17.
- **Seize**: a unit "takes possession of a designated area by using overwhelming force. An enemy force can no longer place
  direct fire on a seized objective." Once seized, the force "clears the terrain within that objective" — FM 3-90 (2023),
  para B-57.
- **Secure**: a unit "prevents the enemy from damaging or destroying a force, facility, or geographical location" — FM
  3-90 (2023), para B-56.
- **Isolate**: a unit "seals off an enemy, physically and psychologically, from sources of support and denies it freedom
  of movement" — FM 3-90 (2023), para B-51.
- **What "cleared" means for a building:** "all the rooms and hallways, but also any stairs, basements, crawl spaces, and
  the roof." The "thoroughness of the search" is set by the mission — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), app. C introduction (pdf
  p. 243).
- Clearing implies holding: "the clearing of a building should include the securing of the building to prevent enemy
  forces from returning to areas already cleared", with back-clearing as time permits — ATTP 3-06.11 (2011), para
  7-145.
- A room is clear when the team has eliminated the threats, occupied the points of domination and "establishes control
  of the room" — ATP 3-06.11 (2024), para C-78. The drill's end state is "CLEAR" reported, security set, the entry point
  marked, and the squad reorganized — TC 3-21.76 (2025), drill 07-SQD-D9509, steps 7–10.
- Counterattack readiness is part of the standard: "maintains a sufficient fighting force to repel an enemy
  counterattack" — TC 3-21.76 (2025), drill 07-SQD-D9509, Standards.
- **Numbers: none.** Doctrine gives no quantitative success threshold for an urban attack (e.g. % of rooms cleared,
  casualties inflicted). ATP 3-06 (2022), Table 6-1 gives example measures of effectiveness only for stability tasks.

#### 0. Bottom line first

Two independent historical-analysis programmes compared urban attacks with open-terrain attacks using matched data: The Dupuy Institute (TDI) for the US Army's Center for Army Analysis (CAA), 2002–04, and Dstl (UK), 2023, which also re-confirms 1980s UK DOAC/DOAE work. **Neither finds that urban terrain lowers the attacker's chance of success. Both find that urban terrain slows the attack and lengthens it.** On casualties, both find that urban fighting is *no worse* for the attacker per day, and if anything better.

The doctrinal 9:1–15:1 figure (ATP 3-06 Table 5-1) is a planning table "adapted from ATP 2-01.3, Table B-16" (from this repo's own `docs/urban-doctrine.md`). I found no historical dataset behind it.

Important caveat for all of this data: attackers **chose** which towns to attack and with what force. The historical urban attacks were typically made at favourable ratios against encircled or isolated defenders, so the success rates are conditioned on that selection.

TDI's own conclusions:
- p. 50: "the terrain ... has no significantly measurable influence on the outcome of battle."
- p. 49: terrain could still shift combat power "by 20 to 30 percent" without being detectable in this sample.

- Conclusion slide: "Urban terrain favours the attacker" and "UK forces should not seek to avoid attacking into urban areas."
- **No force ratios are given** in the slides, so this cannot be read as success at a given ratio.

#### 1d. McLaurin, Jureidini, McDonald & Sellers, *Modern Experience in City Combat*, USAHEL Technical Memorandum 5-87 (Abbott Associates), March 1987, DTIC ADA180999 [SEARCH only; DTIC was down]

This is a case study of about 20+ post-1940 city battles. Excerpts surfaced by two separate searches:
- "odds favoring an ultimate attacker victory do not materially increase" once the force advantage exceeds 2:1.
- More force only shortens the time needed to take the city.
- At 4:1 or better in personnel, "even major cities did not consume more than two weeks" on average.
- "Defense in a built-up area does not appear to be a better risk than defense on other terrain".

**Verify before citing.**

#### 4. Defender multiplier for urban terrain (quantitative)

**Dupuy QJM, *Numbers, Predictions and War* (1985 ed.)** [BLOG: Lawrence, TDI, 16 Aug 2022]
- Terrain factor (Table 1, p. 228): **Urban 1.4**; Rolling-Mixed 1.3; Rugged-Mixed 1.5.
- Posture factors (Table 5, p. 230): hasty 1.3, **prepared 1.5**, fortified 1.6.
- Prepared defence in urban terrain therefore comes to 1.5 × 1.4 = **2.1** (range 1.82–2.24).
- So urban terrain is treated as **about the same as rolling or rugged mixed terrain**.
- Lawrence calls the QJM value "a game construct", not validated against urban engagements.

**TDI empirical:**
- No defensive multiplier could be measured. Any effect is under about 20–30% (Phase I, pp. 49–50).
- Lawrence 2022: the defensive value of urban terrain "clearly was not massive" [BLOG].

**Dupuy attrition verity:** in difficult terrain, casualty rates for both sides "decline markedly" [SEARCH].

**Nothing found:**
- no research-based multiplier of 3–5× or higher for urban defence;
- no TNDM urban factor distinct from the QJM 1.4.

What these jointly suggest:
- The research gives **no support for urban attack success being much lower than open-terrain attack success at the same ratio**. The open-terrain band the game uses (3:1 vs prepared, 40–55%) is the best-supported reference, and nothing found argues for going far below it.
- Item 4, taken at face value, puts a prepared urban defence at roughly the same multiplier as rugged ground. That implies at most a modest shift down. It does not support the doctrine's three-fold-plus penalty.
- A game result of **56% at 3:1 is consistent with the historical data**: TDI found no failures above 2.56:1, and Dstl found urban attackers won more often.
- What urban terrain *should* cost the attacker, per all sources, is **time and tempo**, not outcome or casualties: slower advance, longer engagements, fewer penetrations or breakthroughs, and more defenders captured rather than escaping.
- Caveat: every dataset is at division or brigade level, with at most 6 company-level urban cases (Dstl). **No published research gives a company-vs-platoon-in-houses success rate.** Any company-level band is an extrapolation from division and brigade data.

## Research on clearing time (not doctrine), 2026-10-16

Gathered after the author asked whether ATP 3-06.11's 40–60 minutes could be trusted, and for real data before a target is set. The Fallujah quote (1SG Smith, *Eyewitness to War* Vol. II, pp. 199–200) and TC 90-1's lane times were re-checked on the text. Headings below are demoted one level from the research notes.


Research note, 2026-10-16. The question is how long infantry take to clear a room, a house and a block, with what force and against what opposition. The yardstick is the game's resolver: a ~200 m² two-storey house of ~19 rooms, cleared by an 8-man squad against 3 defenders in ~7 min (~20 s per room plus combat). A one-storey ~10-room house takes ~3.6 min.

Tags: **[READ]** I read the primary text (local copy or full page). **[SECONDARY]** a secondary work quoting or summarising the primary. **[SEARCH]** a search-engine summary only, not verified.

Bottom line first: **I found no open-literature study that times building clearing under controlled conditions** (time per room or per building against known opposition). Urban Warrior, Project Metropolis, the MOUT ACTD, Land Warrior / Objective Force Warrior, DBBL, Dstl/DERA, FOI, DSTO and TNO all came up empty. The usable evidence is:
- drill-level timings in seconds (entry into one room);
- one ARL live experiment that gives a duration for a scripted squad attack;
- a handful of combat throughput figures (houses per day), mostly from Fallujah 2004;
- doctrine and training-lane allotments.

Everything above the room level is throughput (houses per unit per day). It includes movement between buildings, searching, consolidation and rest, so it gives an upper bound on time per house, not a measurement of it.

### 4. Summary of what exists and what does not

- **Time per room exists only at drill level**: about 2-3 s to enter and about 7-8 s to finish one room with no follow-on search (Kaya 2024). No study times rooms inside a whole-house clearance.
- **Time per house has no controlled measurement.** The closest are:
  - a scripted Ranger-squad vignette on a 3-4-room section against 2-5 OpFor, about 30 min including the approach (ARL-TR-2583);
  - an unopposed by-the-book police clear that reaches the 2nd floor in about 3 min (DRDC CTA);
  - Fallujah throughput implying roughly 20-80 squad-minutes per house, mostly against light or no resistance after vehicle fires (derived);
  - doctrine's 40-60 / 90 min per one-floor building per platoon (unsourced).
- **Per block per day**: Hue, about one block per battalion-day against NVA in prepared positions (weak, [SEARCH]); Fallujah, of the order of 70-100 houses per company-day.
- **Where casualties occur**: 70-90% outside the building (JRTC/CALL; MOUT ACTD via Glaze). A clearing-time model that charges most of its losses to the interior is off by this much.

**What these suggest together for a ~200 m², two-storey, ~19-room house cleared by an 8-man squad against ~3 defenders:**

1. **Time to move through the rooms** (the resolver's ~20 s per room) agrees with the drill data. Entry plus domination of one room takes about 8 s in the lab. Allowing for corridors, stairs, marking and the next stack, 15-30 s per room is plausible. That gives ~5-10 min of pure movement for 19 rooms. No source contradicts this, and no source measures it directly.
2. **No source supports ~7 min for the whole task** once defenders, searching and consolidation are included. Every figure above the drill level falls in the tens of minutes:
   - ~30 min, scripted Ranger squad on a 3-4-room section against 2-5 OpFor;
   - ~20-80 squad-minutes per Fallujah house, mostly light opposition, derived;
   - 40-90 min for a one-floor building per platoon in doctrine;
   - 30 min for the training lane at full speed.

   The defended, two-storey case sits at or above the top of these. Hue-style strongpoints run from hours to days.
3. **Most of the extra time is probably not in the room-entry drill.** It is in contact itself: the firefight with each defender, reorganising, casualty handling and resupply. It is also in search and consolidation (Fallujah houses were searched for caches; blocks were cleared up to three times), and in the approach, where 70-90% of casualties occur.
